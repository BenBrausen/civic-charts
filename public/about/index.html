About Civic Charts

Civic Charts turns official public data into charts anyone can understand and share, with the source on every one and every number checked against the data.

Why it exists

The numbers that shape everyday life (jobs, prices, rent, wages, health, the weather) are published by government agencies for free, but they're buried in spreadsheets and tables. Meanwhile, the charts that spread online often have no source, a cherry-picked time range, or a number nobody can trace. Civic Charts is built to make the honest version the easiest one to find, understand and share.

  • Trustworthy: straight from the source, with the source shown and linked.
  • Easy to understand: a plain-English takeaway and key facts with every chart.
  • Easy to share: sized for every social platform, as images, videos, slides or a link.
  • Free: no account, no paywall, no ads.

Where the data comes from

Every chart is built from data published by the organization that collects it: mostly U.S. federal agencies such as the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Census Bureau, the Bureau of Economic Analysis, the Department of Housing and Urban Development, the Energy Information Administration, NOAA, the CDC and the FBI. A few come from established research and industry sources (for example the University of Michigan, MIT's Election Lab, the World Bank, Freddie Mac, Zillow and Indeed), and every chart says which.

  • Data is pulled directly from each publisher's official API or files, automatically, every few hours.
  • Each chart names the publisher, the exact dataset or series, the date it was retrieved, and links to the original.
  • Numbers are shown as published. When we calculate something (a percent change, a rank, an inflation adjustment), the chart says what was calculated and how.
  • Every update is saved, so each dataset has a public version history: what the numbers were, and when they changed. It's in our public data folder.

How every chart is checked

Charts are made by code from the data, not written by hand or generated by AI. On top of that, a claim check reads every word on a chart before it can be exported or shared, and blocks it if:

  • a number appears that isn't in the data or its calculated facts,
  • a "now" or "latest" figure doesn't match the most recent value,
  • it says something rose or fell when the data says otherwise, or
  • it calls something a record when it isn't.

It also warns about cause-and-effect wording the data can't support, about tiny counties that can top rankings on a handful of people (they're left out of "highest" and "lowest" lists), and about combining datasets in misleading ways. The source line can be restyled but never removed, and every share link rebuilds the chart from the official data, so anyone can check that what they saw matches the source.

What it can't do: stop someone from editing an image elsewhere, or catch an error the publisher made. If a source revises its data, the chart updates with it.

How to get the most from it

  • Find a chart: search or browse by topic. Every chart also has its own page, like civiccharts.com/gas-price/.
  • Look local: pick your state, and state charts highlight it and compare it with the U.S. Many maps cover every county too.
  • Read before sharing: each chart's "How to read it" and "What this shows" notes explain what the number does and doesn't mean.
  • Share it: choose a size for your platform and download an image, video or slides, with post text ready to copy.
  • Follow along: new data posts on Bluesky, Threads, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube and TikTok (@civiccharts), and in the RSS feed.

Corrections

If something's wrong, we want to know: email corrections@civiccharts.com. We review every report, fix what's wrong, and list every correction to a published chart below, with the date and what changed.

Reuse and citing

Charts, images, videos and text you make here are yours to share and publish, including commercially (see the terms). Please keep the source line, so readers can see where the numbers come from. To cite a chart, use its page, for example:

Civic Charts. "Unemployment Rate by State." Data: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. https://civiccharts.com/state-unemployment/ (accessed today).

Each chart page has a Cite this chart button that fills this in.
